**Goal:** Explore a single mechanic in isolation, away from the pressure of a full game, so I could focus on how it felt moment to moment.

**Why it mattered:** I wanted to practise documenting my process as I went, rather than reconstructing it afterwards from memory.

I focused on:

- Keeping the prototype small enough to **finish quickly**
- Making small **tuning changes** clearly noticeable in testing
- Capturing **screenshots and notes** at every stage

## Decisions I made along the way

<div class="card">

**The core approach:** I prototyped two very different versions of the main interaction before picking one.

- **Option A** felt more intuitive to new players, but had a low skill ceiling.
- **Option B** took longer to learn, but rewarded precision in a way that felt more satisfying once it clicked.

**Pacing:** I iterated on difficulty escalation several times based on playtester reactions.

- First instinct: ramp difficulty quickly.
- What playtesters showed me: most people were still learning the core interaction well past when I expected mastery.
- Final approach: a noticeably slower ramp than my first instinct.

</div>

## What I learned

The biggest lesson from this project was how much playtesting changes my own assumptions. Several decisions that felt obvious to me going in turned out to be genuinely confusing for first-time players.

I also came away with a better sense of how to scope a prototype. Keeping it tightly focused on one mechanic made it much easier to actually finish and learn something from.
